Housing Affordability

How has it become harder to buy a home in America? This project examines four decades of U.S. housing price data against median household income to reveal structural shifts in affordability and striking parallels to the period before the 2008 financial crisis.

Python Pandas Data Visualization FRED Data EDA

What's the Problem?

Housing affordability has become a major economic and social problem in the United States. This project explores how U.S. housing affordability has changed over time, and what different housing price metrics reveal about broader market dynamics. Rather than just relying on a single affordability measure, this analysis examines how various price metrics provide a layered insight into market structure, trends, and shifts.

Understanding these relationships is important for first-time buyers aiming to manage financial risk, for policymakers working to address supply shortages, and for economists seeking to gauge economic health and forecast the market. By analyzing how income growth compares to changes in housing prices, this project aims to determine whether purchasing power has kept pace with the rising costs of homeownership.

The Data

This analysis uses four publicly available datasets from the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) database. In the raw data, each row represents a specific point in time — ranging from quarterly to yearly observations depending on the dataset — for a given economic indicator. The features explored are:

  • Median Sales Price of Houses Sold
  • Average Sales Price of Houses Sold
  • All-Transactions House Price Index (HPI)
  • Median Household Income

The housing datasets are reported quarterly, while income is reported annually. While the initial files varied in size — with some housing data containing over 250 rows stretching back to 1963 — the final merged dataset focuses on the intersecting period from 1984 to 2024.

One notable assumption is that these national aggregates accurately reflect the broader economic reality; this inherently masks regional real estate variations. While the raw values are nominal, the analysis focuses on the ratio of housing costs to income, which controls for inflation by measuring the relative purchasing power of a median household over time.

Cleaning & Prep

To ensure comparability across datasets, observation date columns were converted to datetime format and a year variable was extracted. Since housing prices and HPI are reported quarterly while income is annual, quarterly values were grouped by year and averaged to produce consistent annual estimates — preventing artificially inflated values and maintaining alignment with the income dataset's reporting frequency.

An inner join was performed across all four dataframes using year as the key. A major trade-off was sacrificing pre-1984 housing data to ensure every row had a corresponding income value for accurate affordability ratio calculations.

New variables created include:

  • Median price-to-income ratio — how many years of income are needed to buy a median-priced house (standard affordability)
  • Average price-to-income ratio — how many years of income are needed to buy an average-priced house (affordability including the high-end market)
  • HPI-to-income ratio — how housing prices have changed relative to income over time (affordability trend / asset appreciation)

What Is the Data Telling Us?

Visuals are the most intuitive way to see the complex relationship between housing costs and income over the last four decades.

Here you can see the widening gap between the median and average home price. It clearly shows how the high-end real estate market is disproportionately driving up the average market price. Looking at both the gap and the divergence visuals together, there is a clear similarity between the market dynamics around 2007 and today, suggesting that like 20 years ago, today's average market is being heavily influenced by high-end real estate.

Affordability ratios directly measure how many times median income would be required to purchase a typical home. This again shows a striking similarity between the gap in price-to-income from before the 2008 financial crisis and today. On top of the market dynamic similarity, the gap between what a person must earn to buy a median versus an average home is also mirrored — suggesting that an average person today is in a very similar financial situation as they would have been 20 years ago, right before the 2008 financial crisis.

Plotting the House Price Index against income reveals purchasing power trends directly. A rising ratio means house prices are increasing faster than income — a decline in affordability. A falling ratio means housing prices are increasing slower than income — an increase in affordability. This visualization again mirrors prior trends, illustrating the same sharp increase in the ratio today as two decades earlier, revealing that people today cannot afford homes because their income is growing much slower than house prices.

Together, these visualizations directly answer the research question: housing has become structurally less affordable relative to income, and relying solely on average prices paints a skewed picture of the typical buyer's reality.

What Does the Data Mean?

This analysis reveals that U.S. housing affordability has deteriorated, with median income growth severely lagging behind housing price appreciation. While both income and housing prices have increased over time, housing prices have grown at a faster rate — particularly in recent decades — making it significantly more difficult to afford a home today.

The price-to-income ratio shows a clear upward trend, indicating that purchasing a home today requires a greater number of annual household incomes than before. The House Price Index also rises more steeply than income in recent years, suggesting asset appreciation has outpaced wage growth and reduced purchasing power for new entrants to the market. The widening gap between average and median sales prices further implies increasing market imbalance, potentially reflecting high-end growth or wealth concentration at the upper end of the housing market.

One of the most striking insights is the uncanny similarity between today's real estate market and the market right before the 2008 financial crisis. The data suggests that, at least within the scope of this analysis, people are in the same position as they were 20 years ago near the Great Recession. However, it would be misleading to conclude the markets are identical — this analysis is high-level and does not capture the granular details (renting, mortgages, regulatory context) that would provide further insight into the market's health.

Limits & Reflection

Because the data consists of national aggregates, it does not account for regional variation in housing markets — which can differ substantially across states and metropolitan areas. The datasets also do not incorporate inflation adjustments, mortgage interest rates, or other market context, all of which affect affordability in practice.

Median income does not reflect wealth or savings, which also influence home-buying ability. This study focuses solely on home purchase affordability and does not examine rental markets, which would provide a more holistic view of the housing landscape.

In essence, this analysis provides a broad structural view of how housing costs compare to income over time. Valuable next steps would be to incorporate market context (mortgage rates, policy changes), add the rental market, and break the data down geographically by state or city to develop a deeper understanding of affordability and today's real estate market as a whole.
